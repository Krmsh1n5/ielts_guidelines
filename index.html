    My IELTS preparation notes

    My IELTS preparation, one problem at a time.

    Hi, I’m Dmitriy Kuramshin. I made this website to keep a clear record of how I prepared for IELTS and to share the methods that were useful to me.

    At first, my plan was simple: do as many mock tests as possible. After some time, I noticed that my scores were not improving consistently and sometimes even went backwards. I changed the way I prepared. Instead of treating the exam as one big task, I broke it into smaller problems and worked on them one by one.

    This site follows that process. It includes my own essay versions, including cases where I rewrote the same essay several times to improve it, as well as the techniques I used for Reading, Listening, Speaking, and Writing. I also kept the small rules, collocations, spelling checks, and answer patterns that I wanted to remember.

    This is not a complete IELTS course, and I do not expect every method here to work for everyone. A pattern that feels natural to me may feel awkward to someone else. I think the useful approach is to try a technique, keep what helps, and change what does not.

    I also found it important to build my own system. For me, that included a physical notebook where I wrote down what I learned, even when the same information was saved digitally. The point of this website is similar: it shows my preparation as it actually developed, so you can take ideas from it and adapt them to your own way of learning.

    General & timing

    IELTS Academic Reading gives you 60 minutes for 3 sections and 40 questions. There is no extra answer-transfer time, so the hour has to cover reading, answering, transferring, and checking.

    The passages are not equally demanding in practice. I usually found Passage 3 the hardest, although occasionally Passage 2 was the section that consumed the most time. Because of that, I did not treat 20 - 20 - 20 as a fixed rule.

    My timing strategy

    Finish the easier material quickly enough to protect time for the difficult passage and revision. The exact split should respond to the test in front of you rather than a rigid timer.

    Passage 1: 10-15 minMove efficiently and avoid overchecking easy questions.
    Passage 2: 15-20 minAllow more time if the language or matching tasks are dense.
    Passage 3 + reviewUse the remaining time for the hardest passage and unanswered questions.

    Question-first or passage-first?

    One of the most valuable decisions in Reading is made before you start reading deeply: determine whether the question type lets you use the passage's order as a map.

    When order can guide you

    Read the questions first, identify the information you need, then move through the passage and answer as you locate it.

    • Multiple choice
    • True / False / Not Given and Yes / No / Not Given often behave this way in practice
    • Matching sentence endings
    • Sentence completion
    • Short-answer questions

    When broader understanding matters

    Do not expect a clean question-by-question route. You may need to understand paragraph purpose, compare several mentions of the same person, or scan across the passage.

    • Matching headings
    • Matching information
    • Matching features
    • Many summary, table, flow-chart, and diagram tasks
    Use answered questions as location anchors

    If you have confidently located the answers to Questions 4 and 6 in an order-based set, the answer to Question 5 will often be between those two locations. This can shrink a full passage into a much smaller search area.

    Matching features needs a different mindset. A researcher or author can appear several times and express several ideas. Finding the person's name only tells you where to read. You still need to decide which statement is actually supported.

    Treat order as a strategy, not a universal law. Where IELTS explicitly states that a task follows text order, that is noted in the Question Types section above.

    High-value strategies

    The best Reading tactics reduce the amount of text you need to process at once. They help you locate meaning, reject unsupported options, and protect time when one question becomes unproductive.

    01

    Map synonyms and paraphrases

    When a question is difficult to follow, isolate its key meaning and look for equivalent wording in the passage. IELTS frequently changes the vocabulary or grammar rather than repeating the question verbatim.

    Question: declined sharply → Passage might say: fell dramatically · Question: was responsible for → Passage might say: led to / caused / resulted in
    02

    Separate contradiction from missing information

    For TFNG and YNNG, False / No means the passage contradicts the statement. Not Given means the passage does not establish the statement either way. Missing information is not a contradiction.

    03

    Use neighboring answers to narrow the search

    In an order-based question set, a confirmed answer gives you a location marker. Two confirmed answers can create boundaries around an unanswered question and save repeated scanning.

    04

    Skip and return when focus becomes too narrow

    If a question is consuming too much time, move to the next one or even the next passage and return later. This is a technique I have used for years. Over-concentration can make tiny wording details feel more important than the passage's larger meaning.

    05

    Maximise solved questions before perfecting one answer

    Your score is based on correct answers. Spending several minutes on one stubborn item can cost easier marks later. Make a provisional choice or leave a clear marker, continue, then use revision time to return.

    The TFNG/YNNG distinction follows the official IELTS task definitions: contradiction and absence of information are scored differently.

    How to practise

    Do not prepare only for the question types that happen to appear in your most recent practice books. Build enough exposure that an unusual task does not change your strategy on exam day.

    Practise every task type at least twice

    I would include less common-looking tasks such as diagram labels and flow charts. I had not encountered Matching Headings in roughly four or five recent Cambridge books I practised with, but then saw it in both a mock test and my official IELTS exam.

    Deliberately use harder texts

    Include passages with dense grammar, abstract arguments, unfamiliar vocabulary, and long sentence structures. Easy passages are useful for technique, but they are a poor test of how well your strategy survives under real difficulty.

    Do not calibrate only from easy mock scores

    I was averaging Band 9 in Reading during mock practice. A difficult Passage 3 in the real exam challenged me much more, and my final Reading score was Band 8. The lesson for me was to practise difficulty, not just accuracy.

    Train the skip-and-return habit

    Skipping should not be an emergency decision you invent during the exam. Practise it in timed sets so that you know when to leave a question, how to mark it, and how much time to reserve for returning.

    Preparation rule

    Coverage first, then difficulty. Learn how every question type behaves, and then test those strategies on passages that are harder than your comfortable practice level.

    The four-part map

    Officially, IELTS Listening has four parts with ten questions each. Parts 1 and 2 use everyday social situations; Parts 3 and 4 move into educational and training contexts. The recording is played once, so preparation time matters.

    Part 1

    Everyday conversation

    Two speakers, often exchanging practical details. In my practice this was where precise typed information such as names, numbers, addresses, times and short form entries mattered most.

    Part 2

    Everyday monologue

    One speaker in a social context. I often prepared for maps, multiple choice and matching here, where understanding the option list before the audio was especially useful.

    Part 3

    Educational discussion

    Two main speakers, sometimes with a tutor. I treated multiple choice and matching as high-attention tasks because ideas are paraphrased and speakers can refine or disagree with one another.

    Part 4

    Academic monologue

    One speaker on an academic subject. In my practice, note/table/summary-style completion made exact listening and fast typing particularly important.

    My preparation pattern, not an IELTS rule

    I trained around a simple distinction: Parts 1 and 4 often felt like type what you hear, while Parts 2 and 3 often felt like choose or match what the speaker means. IELTS does not permanently assign question types to specific parts, so I use this as a preparation pattern rather than a guarantee.

    Official reference: IELTS Academic Listening test format. The official sample bank also shows that task types can appear in different parts.

    Type exactly what you hear

    My biggest avoidable mistake was changing singular and plural forms on my own. I would hear one form, then “correct” it while typing because another form seemed more grammatical. That is dangerous.

    Do not rewrite the recording in your head

    If you hear stone, do not automatically type stones. If the task asks for words from the recording, IELTS states that you do not have to change those words. Listen to endings instead of predicting them.

    01

    Listen through the final consonant

    Do not mentally stop once you recognise the root word. The final -s, -ed, or other ending may decide whether the answer is correct.

    02

    Use grammar to predict, not to overwrite

    Before the audio, grammar can tell you that a noun, verb, adjective or plural form is likely. Once the speaker gives the answer, however, the audio outranks your prediction.

    03

    Separate hearing from typing

    Train short dictation until entering a word does not consume all your attention. The faster spelling becomes automatic, the less likely you are to miss the next answer while finishing the current one.

    04

    Part 4 still needs exactness

    An academic monologue may sound conceptually harder, but completion questions can still be lost through one letter, one ending or one extra word. Do not let the topic distract you from the answer format.

    IELTS explicitly warns that incorrect spelling and grammar can lose marks, and its completion-task guidance tells candidates to keep within the stated word/number limit.

    Selection & matching

    The questions I treat with the most caution are “choose two from five/six” and matching tasks. They are less about catching one isolated word and more about tracking meaning across a set of alternatives.

    Choose two from a longer list

    Do not expect the audio to walk through options A, B, C, D in the same visible order. The question itself progresses with the recording, but the option list can be referenced, rejected, corrected or paraphrased in a different sequence.

    • Read every option before the relevant audio.
    • Mark the semantic difference between similar options.
    • Keep listening after one plausible choice; the speaker may qualify it.
    • Confirm the required number of answers before the recording starts.

    Matching

    My preference is to read the answer options on the right first when the left-side items are names or topics that the speaker is likely to identify explicitly. The difficult part is usually recognizing the summarized or paraphrased description.

    • Learn the options, not only the labels.
    • Expect paraphrase rather than exact repetition.
    • Track speaker changes carefully in Part 3.
    • Do not select an option only because you heard one matching keyword.
    Order: an important distinction

    IELTS says the questions follow the order of information in the recording. That does not mean a list of answer options will be discussed in alphabetical or visual order. For choose-two and matching tasks, I prepare to evaluate each option independently.

    Read ahead & predict

    The greatest time-management advantage I found was using every available reading window. I try to understand as many upcoming questions as possible before their recording begins, especially because Parts 3 and 4 can contain denser wording.

    01

    Classify the missing information

    Ask what the gap requires: a noun, verb, adjective, number, date, street name, price, time, person, place, or another category. This narrows what your ear needs to catch.

    Example: “The course begins on ___.” → expect a date/day. “The main reason was ___.” → probably a noun phrase. “Students must ___ the form.” → expect a verb.
    02

    Mentally fill the gap with a plausible answer

    Your invented answer is not a guess to submit. It is a listening primer. If you imagine what kind of phrase would make the sentence work, you are more prepared to recognize the real answer when the speaker says it.

    03

    Read beyond the current part when you genuinely have time

    In practice, I could sometimes reach the Part 3 questions before the silent reading periods around Part 1 were over. That meant later sections felt less rushed and I was less likely to lose a question because I had never processed its wording.

    04

    Do not go so far ahead that you abandon the present

    If you notice that your attention has drifted too far into later sections, return to the questions you are about to answer and read them again more carefully. Reading ahead only helps if the current set is already understood.

    This prediction method is also reflected in an official IELTS teaching article, which recommends anticipating likely answer types and deliberately practising number-rich listening: Develop listening skills for IELTS.

    Numbers, formats & spelling

    Part 1 rewards tiny pieces of precision. Names, phone numbers, prices, dates and times are easy to underestimate because the language is simple. I treat them as a separate skill and drill them until the format is automatic.

    Word limits

    Read the instruction literally. If it says NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER, exceeding that maximum loses the mark. IELTS states that hyphenated forms such as check-in count as one word and contractions are not tested.

    Numbers & phone details

    Practise common spoken forms until they do not surprise you. In phone numbers, zero may be spoken as “oh”. Listen for repetitions, corrections and digit grouping rather than trying to remember the whole number before typing.

    Times & dates

    Identify exactly what the answer box already provides and what still needs to be entered. Train both spoken forms and written forms so 7.30 p.m., a date, or a day name does not require extra thinking during the recording.

    Money & decimals

    Listen carefully for units and words such as point. Know the visual difference between a decimal such as 2.5 and a thousands separator such as 2,500. Follow the task's visible format instead of inventing extra punctuation or units.

    There is no substitute for the official task instructions

    Do not memorise one universal notation as if it overrides the task in front of you. Read the instruction and the surrounding form or table first. Official sample tasks expose you to real layouts, including times and prices, so use them to practise format recognition.

    When spelling freezes under stress

    One technique from my English teacher helped me on exam day: compare the possible spellings and use both meaning and visual familiarity instead of overthinking the word.

    • unimportant / inimportant → the second form simply looks wrong.
    • ankle / uncle / ancle → uncle is a relative, ancle looks wrong, so ankle.
    • swimming has double m; boxing has one x.

    Know the rule, then trust trained memory

    Rules about doubled consonants and suffixes are useful, but during a once-only recording there may be no time to reconstruct every spelling rule. Repeated exposure should make common words look and feel familiar enough to type quickly.

    • Learn common spelling patterns.
    • Drill names and letter-by-letter dictation.
    • Review your personal spelling errors after every mock.
    • Do not change a correct first instinct without a concrete reason.

    A small investment can protect easy marks

    I mastered many of these Part 1 conventions only one day before my exam, and it did not take long. That made the lesson obvious to me: do not be lazy about details that can be trained quickly.

    Attention & practice loop

    You cannot replay the official recording. That makes sustained attention a trainable exam skill, not just a personality trait. I improved it by doing enough timed Listening tests that losing focus became something I could diagnose and correct.

    Never sacrifice the next answer for the previous one

    If you miss a word, make the best provisional choice you can and rejoin the recording. Mentally replaying one missed answer while the audio continues can turn one lost mark into three.

    Classify every mistake

    After a mock, I separate mistakes into categories: spelling, singular/plural, number or format, failed prediction, paraphrase/matching, or lost attention. The category tells me what to practise next.

    Repeat until the score is stable

    When I performed poorly on a mock, I did another test, then another, until I could reach roughly Band 8.5–9 performance again. The purpose was not to collect tests; it was to make concentration and recovery repeatable.

    Train the whole test, not isolated clips only

    Short dictation is excellent for spelling and numbers, but full mocks train the attention shift from easy social detail to denser academic discussion and monologue without a reset.

    My listening loop

    Timed test → classify every wrong answer → drill the weak category → repeat under full-test conditions. Every task can be mastered, but only if the error itself becomes specific.

    My core speaking method

    My biggest improvements did not come from memorising more advanced vocabulary. They came from taking an idea I could already explain and producing it again with cleaner sentence structure, more natural collocations, fewer grammar errors, consistent pronouns, one clear example or explanation, and a controlled ending.

    General — test format

    The Speaking test is a recorded face-to-face interview lasting 11–14 minutes. Part 1 is an introduction and interview on familiar topics and lasts about 4–5 minutes. Part 2 is the long turn: you have 1 minute to prepare, then speak for 1–2 minutes; the whole part lasts about 3–4 minutes including preparation and brief follow-up. Part 3 is a more general and abstract discussion related to Part 2 and lasts about 4–5 minutes. The Speaking test is the same for Academic and General Training candidates.

    Your performance is assessed throughout the test using four equally weighted criteria: Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, and Pronunciation. The final Speaking band is based on the combined performance across the full interview, not on a separate score for each part.

    Pronunciation is assessed through intelligibility and control of features such as stress, rhythm, and intonation. You do not need a native accent; what matters is that your speech can be understood without undue effort.

    Parts 1 → 2 → 3 — how they escalate

    Part 1 is personal and concrete. My aim is to answer directly, then add a reason or a useful detail without stretching the answer unnecessarily. Short, controlled answers are better for me than long answers that become difficult to manage grammatically.

    Part 2 is the long turn. After 1 minute of preparation, I use the task-card points as a guide rather than a script. Real memories or experiences are usually easier for me to develop naturally because I can add details even if I forget a prepared phrase. The priority is to keep the talk organised and sustained for the required time.

    Part 2 — opening phrases by cue card type. A prepared opening phrase lets you buy time, calm down, and begin fluently rather than starting mid-thought. The phrase should match the card type:

    Person: "There are several people who have had a real impact on my life, but if I had to single out one, it would definitely be…"
    Event / memorable day: "There were a lot of memorable moments during my time at university, but if I had to choose one, I would definitely emphasise the day when…"
    Place: "I have been lucky enough to visit a number of interesting places, but one that genuinely stood out was…"
    Object / technology: "There are quite a few things I use on a daily basis, but one that I genuinely could not manage without is…"
    Skill / achievement: "I have tried to develop a number of skills over the years, but the one that has made the biggest difference to me is probably…"
    Film / book: "I consume a fair amount of content in my spare time, but one title that left a real impression on me was…"

    Part 3 moves to more general and abstract discussion. I develop answers more fully here by stating a position, explaining the reason, and extending it with another reason or example when useful. Part 3 gives more opportunity to show analysis and extended speaking, but the final band still reflects performance across the whole Speaking test.

    Tips & strategies
    Self-correct only when it is quick. Occasional self-correction is compatible with high-band performance, but repeated reformulation and language-search hesitation can disrupt fluency. If I know the correction immediately, I make it and continue; otherwise I keep the answer moving.
    Use thinking phrases sparingly. Brief phrases such as "That's an interesting question" can give me a moment to organise the next idea, but I do not stack fillers or formal linking phrases. IELTS rewards appropriate, natural cohesion rather than the frequency of discourse markers.
    If you go blank: restate the question in your own words — "So you're asking whether… I think the key issue here is…". This restores coherence and buys thinking time without a visible pause.
    Do not memorise full answers. I memorise structures, useful chunks, and opening patterns instead. A memorised script can sound formulaic or fail to fit the exact question, whereas genuine content is easier to adapt and extend.
    Extend without rambling. Give a reason → follow with mechanism ("which means that…") → add a brief example ("for instance, when I…") → link back ("so that's why I think…"). Four sentences per point is usually enough. Stop and move on.
    How I practice — tips and approach

    The most valuable single improvement you can make is simplifying your sentences. When a sentence becomes a chain of connected thoughts, break it. State the idea in one sentence. Give the reason or consequence in the next. Then use a linking word or phrase — "as a result," "this means that," "because of this" — to reconnect them. The segmentation disappears when the links are natural, and what remains is accurate, fluent speech rather than a single overcomplicated sentence that collapses under its own grammar.

    Speaking naturally matters as much as vocabulary. Sentences learned by heart sound learned by heart — examiners notice. Talking about real experiences, actual opinions, and genuine examples means you can elaborate even if you forget a collocation, because the underlying idea is genuinely yours. The practice method that helped me most: ChatGPT Live Voice Mode. I would give an answer, ask it to evaluate it, list every lexical and grammatical error I made, and suggest improvements. I repeated the same question until I reached Band 7.5–8, then moved on. This targets the underlying fluency problem rather than vocabulary gaps.

    Structure for each part
    Part 1 — 1 idea · 2 reasons or pieces of evidence · 1 example. That is usually enough. Add a second idea only if it connects logically or adds a clear layer of depth. Avoid trailing off into a third unrelated point.
    Part 2 (Cue card) — Start with a general opening phrase to buy time and settle your thoughts after the 1-minute prep. Something like: "There were a lot of memorable days during my senior year at university. However, if I had to choose one, I would definitely emphasise the day when…" This is especially useful when you have limited material. Do not try to remember many collocations — structure your answer clearly and aim for 3–4 high-level phrases well placed. Fluency and addressing the prompts matter more than collocation density.
    Part 3 — Position → Reason 1 (elaborate) → Reason 2 (elaborate) → repeat the pattern once or twice depending on what the question allows → short conclusion. Do not stop to think of the perfect collocation mid-flow. A slightly simpler phrase spoken fluently beats a sophisticated one delivered hesitantly.

    Official scoring criteria

    IELTS Speaking is assessed across Fluency & Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range & Accuracy, and Pronunciation. This site deliberately does not reproduce the official descriptor wording. Use the official documents for the authoritative criteria: Band Descriptors PDF · Key Assessment Criteria PDF.

    Band 8–9: consistently fluent, precise, flexible and easy to understand, with strong control of complex language.
    Band 7: sustained speaking with clear development, useful range and generally reliable control, despite occasional errors.
    Band 6: communication is generally effective, but hesitation, repetition, vocabulary limits or grammar errors are more noticeable.
    Band 5: meaning is usually recoverable, but limited range and control regularly affect flow, precision or clarity.
    Fluency & Coherence

    Can you keep going, and do your ideas connect logically? IELTS looks at the amount and type of hesitation, repetition and self-correction, as well as how naturally you use discourse markers and cohesive features. The goal is not to insert more linkers, but to keep the answer coherent without sounding mechanical.

    Lexical Resource

    Do you have range, and can you paraphrase? At Band 7, you use less common vocabulary appropriately and successfully paraphrase when you don't know or can't access a word. At Band 8, your word choice is flexible and precise. A wrong word used confidently scores lower than a simpler word used correctly.

    Grammatical Range & Accuracy

    The same principle as writing: range is only valuable when accuracy holds. The key structures for Band 7–8 in speaking are the same as in writing — conditionals, concessive clauses, relative clauses — but spoken naturally rather than constructed. My fix: simplify the sentence first, make it accurate, then try to add one complex element.

    Pronunciation

    Not accent imitation, but intelligibility. The examiner listens for whether stress, rhythm, intonation and connected speech help communicate meaning. A non-native accent is not a problem by itself; the important question is whether it makes the listener work harder to understand you.

    Accurate natural collocations and reusable sentence control beat forced "advanced" vocabulary. Use in two layers: drill the high-yield phrases first until they are automatic, then use the topic bank as reference.

    The core idea

    Task 1 and Task 2 need different thinking. Task 1 is objective reporting: describe what the visual shows, select the key features, and do not add outside explanations or personal opinions. Task 2 is argument writing: answer every part of the prompt, maintain a clear position where the task requires one, and develop ideas rather than listing them.

    My default Task 2 architecture is PEEL: Point → Explanation → Example → Link back to the prompt. I prefer one developed idea to several shallow ideas. For opinion and outweigh questions, I use a single-position strategy: choose the position I can defend most clearly, state it in the introduction, and keep it consistent throughout the essay.

    My timing target is 20 minutes for Task 1 and 40 minutes for Task 2. I reserve the final few minutes for a targeted grammar scan: articles, subject–verb agreement, plural endings, prepositions, and sentence boundaries. This is more useful to me than trying to add sophisticated vocabulary at the end.

    No surprises in the conclusion. The final paragraph only synthesises what the essay has already established. I do not introduce a new argument, statistic, example, or solution there.

    For coherence, I avoid mechanical transitions. Rather than starting every paragraph with “First”, “Secondly”, or “Turning to”, I prefer a thematic bridge that grows out of the argument itself. I also avoid repeating the same core phrase throughout a paragraph when a precise pronoun or synonym can carry the reference naturally.

## Step 1 — Score Each Criterion

Score all four criteria against the IELTS 0–9 descriptors. For this practice tool, half-band estimates may be used when helpful, but label them as estimates rather than official criterion awards.

### Criterion 1 — Task Achievement

- Does the overview/summary capture the main trend(s) and most significant feature(s)?
- Are all key features reported with accurate data? Are there omissions or over-detail?
- Is there any irrelevant description or personal opinion?
- Is data compared where comparison is meaningful?
- Is the overview a separate paragraph placed after the introduction (not at the end)?

---

### Criterion 2 — Coherence & Cohesion

Examine at two levels: **whole-text** and **within-paragraph**.

- **Progression**: Does information flow logically paragraph by paragraph?
- **Paragraphing**: Is there a clear intro → overview → body structure?
- **Cohesive devices**: Are linking words (however, furthermore, in contrast…) used accurately and not over/under-used?
- **Referencing**: Are pronouns (it, this, they) and substitution used clearly without ambiguity?
- **Overview placement**: Is the overview paragraph clearly separate from the introduction and body?

---

### Criterion 3 — Lexical Resource

- **Range**: Does the candidate go beyond basic vocabulary? Is there chart-specific lexis (peaked, plateaued, fluctuated, marginally, substantially)?
- **Accuracy**: Identify spelling errors, wrong word form (noun/verb/adj confusion), incorrect collocations, and register mismatches.
- **Sophistication**: Are less common words used appropriately, or do attempts at sophistication produce errors?
- For each error found, quote the original and provide a correction/upgrade.

---

### Criterion 4 — Grammatical Range & Accuracy

- **Range**: Note structures used — simple, compound, complex (relative clauses, passive constructions, comparative structures, participle phrases, etc.)
- **Accuracy**: Mark every error. Categories: subject-verb agreement, article use (a/an/the/zero), tense consistency (past tense for historical data), preposition use, sentence fragments, run-on sentences.
- For each error, quote the original sentence, identify the error type, and give the corrected version.
- Note the error density: high, medium, or low relative to text length.

    Articles and countability
    Zero article for general abstract nouns: Economic growth requires investment in infrastructure. Avoid adding the unless the noun is made specific.
    Singular countable nouns need a determiner: ✗ in industrial refining plant → ✓ in an industrial refining plant.
    Uncountable traps: information, evidence, research, progress, knowledge, equipment, advice, infrastructure are normally treated as uncountable.
    Agreement and modifiers
    Distant subject: ignore the intervening prepositional phrase when choosing the verb. ✓ The operation of engines in these vehicles emits carbon dioxide.
    Adverb + adjective: ✗ more financial flexible → ✓ more financially flexible.
    Relative clauses: defining clauses usually take no commas; non-defining clauses are separated with commas: The museum, which was refurbished in 2010, attracted more visitors.
    Task 1 tense and passive voice
    Historical data: keep detail paragraphs in the past simple. The chart itself can still be introduced in the present: The chart compares…

    Task 1 / Bar Chart
    Bar Chart

    My bar-chart pattern is to group categories by behaviour: the two extreme frequency groups together, then the moderate-frequency groups that fluctuate more. I compare the same categories across years rather than describe each bar independently.

    Task context — paraphrased

    Compare how often people in the United States ate fast food in 2003, 2006, and 2013, focusing on the most common frequencies and the main changes across the three years.

    How I approach it

    In my fast-food bar-chart attempts, I grouped the categories by behaviour. The two extreme categories, eating fast food every day and never eating it, are treated together because they stay consistently low. The moderate-frequency categories form the second analytical group because they fluctuate more.

    The overview became stronger when I stopped giving a vague statement about the whole distribution and instead named the extreme ends of the spectrum, the moderate consumers, and the two most common habits. That shift is visible between my Band 7 and Band 7.5 versions.

    In the body, I keep related categories together and compare the same category across years. This matches the structure I actually used rather than a generic “highest bar / lowest bar” template.

    I still check exact data language: proportion is safer than number when the chart is in percentages, and changes such as 2% or 5% must match what the visual actually shows.

    My pattern from the fast-food essay
    ParaPurposeWhat I write
    IntroParaphraseState what the chart compares, the population, and the three years.
    OverviewExtremes + dominant habitsIdentify the consistently low extreme categories, the more variable moderate consumers, and the most common frequencies.
    Body 1Extreme / low-frequency groupDaily, never, and a few-times-a-year categories, compared across the years.
    Body 2Moderate-frequency groupSeveral-times-a-week, once-a-week, and once/twice-a-month categories, with their fluctuations and recovery.
    My overview rule: I keep the overview focused on the dominant pattern, the most striking contrast, or the overall direction, and usually leave detailed figures for the body paragraphs. This is my writing strategy, not an IELTS rule that forbids numbers in an overview.

    Rising vs. falling, highest vs. lowest, early period vs. later period. The grouping is the logic of the report.
    2

    3

    If the unit is a raw count or currency, use "recorded", "reached", "stood at". "Accounted for" implies a share of a total.

    Common Errors to Watch
    • LR-3
      Repeating "increase" and "decrease" in every sentence. Rotate: rose, climbed, surged, fell, declined, dropped, plummeted.
    • GR-4
      "Increased of 20%" — wrong preposition. Verb takes by: increased by 20%. Noun takes of: an increase of 20%.
    • GR-2
      Missing article: "showed increase" → "showed an increase". Every singular countable noun needs a determiner.
    • LR-6
      "The graph rose" — the graph doesn't move. Make the data item the subject: The figure / proportion / share rose.

      Mixing tenses. If the data is historical and complete, stay in past tense throughout the detail paragraphs.

    Task 1 / Line Graph
    Line Graph

    My line-graph pattern is grouping by overall direction: declining countries together and rising countries together, while highlighting peaks, plateaus and final convergence where they matter.

    Task context — paraphrased

    Compare per-person carbon-dioxide emissions in the United Kingdom, Sweden, Italy, and Portugal from 1967 to 2007, with attention to opposite long-term trends and convergence.

    How I approach it

    My CO₂ essay shows the line-graph structure I actually use. I first divide the series by overall direction: the UK and Sweden decline, while Italy and Portugal rise.

    Body 1 follows the declining pair and includes Sweden’s temporary spike. Body 2 follows the rising pair and ends with the convergence of Portugal and Sweden. This keeps the report organised by trend rather than by year.

    The progression from Band 7 to Band 8 is also a reminder that line graphs require data accuracy before sophisticated vocabulary: the later version corrects the 40-year period and the starting order of the UK and Sweden.

    Useful features for me are therefore: starting position, overall direction, a peak or interruption, a plateau, and a final convergence or crossover when one exists.

    Pie Chart

    My pie-chart pattern is to group the two nutrients with similar distributions together and treat added sugar separately because its distribution is different. The report focuses on proportions, not absolute numbers.

    Task context — paraphrased

    Compare how sodium, saturated fat, and added sugar are distributed across breakfast, lunch, dinner, and snacks in a set of typical US meals.

    In my nutrient pie-chart attempts, the overview identifies the dominant meal for each nutrient and the consistently smallest meal. This gives the reader the big picture before any detailed percentages.

    The body grouping is based on similarity of distribution. Sodium and saturated fat are discussed together because their patterns are close; added sugar is separated because its distribution is visibly different.

    The progression from Band 7 to Band 8.5 mainly comes from precision: correcting figures, using proportion / percentage / fraction more naturally, and removing awkward comparisons such as saying one percentage “overcomes” another.

    For pie charts, I avoid the number of unless the task genuinely gives counts. When the visual shows shares of a whole, I use proportion, percentage, share, or fraction.

    Opinion Essay

    For a pure agree/disagree task, I can take a strictly positive or strictly negative position and use both body paragraphs to support it. This practice prompt is a hybrid opinion + second-question task, so its body structure follows the two questions.

    Task context — paraphrased

    Evaluate whether limited punishment can help children learn right from wrong, then explain what kinds of discipline parents and teachers should be allowed to use.

    How I approach it

    For a pure opinion essay such as “To what extent do you agree or disagree?”, I can be strictly positive or strictly negative. I state one clear position in the introduction and use both body paragraphs to develop reasons for that same position.

    The punishment essay in my essay is slightly different because it contains two tasks: first, give the extent of agreement; second, explain what punishments should be allowed. I therefore use Body 1 to develop why limited punishment can help, and Body 2 to answer the second question with acceptable disciplinary methods.

    This is why I do not treat every page labelled “opinion” as having the same body architecture. The command words decide the structure. What stays constant is that my position is explicit from the introduction and remains consistent in the conclusion.

    Inside each body, I develop the point instead of listing: claim → explanation → example or consequence → link back to the question.

    Task 2 / Advantages / Disadvantages
    Advantages / Disadvantages

    Advantages/Disadvantages belongs to the same two-body family as Discuss Both Views: one side per body. The exact wording matters: a plain “advantages and disadvantages” task can be neutral, but “outweigh” requires a clear judgement.

    How I approach it

    I do not currently have a personal Advantages / Disadvantages essay on this site, so I link this type to the closest structure I do have: Discuss Both Views. In both patterns, Body 1 and Body 2 should separately develop the two sides.

    The command word changes the task. If the question only asks for advantages and disadvantages, I can explain both sides without forcing a net judgement. If it asks whether one side outweighs the other, I state which side is stronger in the introduction and keep that judgement consistent.

    I still develop one or two strong points rather than listing many shallow ones. Each body needs explanation and a concrete mechanism or example.

    Task 2 / Cause & Solution
    Cause & Solution

    Cause & Solution uses a strict split in my method: Body 1 explains the cause or causes; Body 2 gives the solution or solutions. I do not mix cause + solution pairs across both paragraphs.

    How I approach it

    My rule is strict: Body 1 = causes; Body 2 = solutions. I do not alternate cause → solution → cause → solution across the two body paragraphs.

    Body 1 explains why the problem exists and develops the mechanism. Body 2 then answers those causes with targeted measures. The solutions should visibly respond to the problem diagnosed in Body 1.

    I do not currently have a personal Cause & Solution essay on this site, so this page does not present another response as mine. I link a external model below, but the structure described here remains my chosen method.

    The conclusion only synthesises the diagnosis and response. It does not introduce a new cause or a third solution.

    Structure & Task Response
    My 4-paragraph pattern — do not mix the bodies
    ParaPurposeWhat I write
    IntroductionDiagnosis + directionParaphrase the problem and signal the main causes and that targeted solutions follow.
    Body 1Causes onlyExplain the cause(s), mechanism, and consequence. No solutions in this paragraph.
    Body 2Solutions onlyGive solution(s) that directly answer the causes from Body 1. No new cause in this paragraph.
    ConclusionSynthesisRestate the main cause(s) and the response. No new information.
    1
    Cause-solution mapping is the key skill
    If Body 1 says: "Cause 1 is poor diet, Cause 2 is sedentary lifestyle" — then Body 2 must say: "Solution 1 targets poor diet by…, Solution 2 targets sedentary lifestyle by…"
    2
    Solutions must have a mechanism
    "Governments should invest in education" is not a solution — it's a slogan. "By subsidising fresh produce while taxing processed foods, governments can change consumer incentives" is a mechanism.
    Language — Causes and Solutions
    Cause language
    This is largely attributable to… / X is primarily driven by… / A key factor is…
    Mechanism
    When X occurs, Y tends to… / This leads to… / As a result of X, Y becomes…
    Solution mapping
    To address this [cause], governments should… / By [mechanism], authorities can directly target…
    Expected result
    This would / could / is likely to… / If implemented, this measure would… — use conditional, not present simple
    Common Errors to Watch
    • TR
      Solutions that don't address the stated causes. If you named "sedentary lifestyle" as a cause, "improving the economy" is not a valid solution.
    • TR
      Introducing new causes in Body 2 that weren't in Body 1. The solution paragraph must map to what was already diagnosed.
    • GR-9
      "If governments will invest…" — wrong conditional form. Use: "If governments invest [present], [problem] will be reduced [future]."

    Task 2 / Two-Part Question
    Two-Part Question

    Two-Part questions are split literally: Body 1 answers Question 1 and Body 2 answers Question 2. My punishment essay is a useful real example because the prompt asks both for an opinion and for acceptable punishment types.

    How I approach it

    For a two-part prompt, I keep the bodies separate: Body 1 answers the first question; Body 2 answers the second. I do not mix answers to both questions inside both paragraphs.

    My punishment essay is the clearest internal example. Its first question asks to what extent I agree that punishment is necessary; its second asks what punishments should be allowed. My first body develops the reasoning behind my agreement, while the second body discusses specific disciplinary methods.

    The introduction can briefly signal both answers, especially when one of the questions asks for an opinion. The conclusion should also reflect both parts without adding another answer.

    If one question is much easier, I still avoid letting it consume nearly the whole essay. The two tasks need visible, developed coverage.

    Task 2 / Positive / Negative Development
    Positive / Negative Development

    I treat Positive / Negative Development as a two-sided evaluation: Body 1 develops one side, Body 2 develops the other, but the introduction and conclusion make the net judgement clear.

    How I approach it

    Positive / Negative Development shares the same two-body architecture as Advantages / Disadvantages and Discuss Both Views: one side or consequence set per body paragraph.

    The key difference is the command. If the question asks whether the development is positive or negative, I must make a net judgement. Body 1 can explain the positive consequences and Body 2 the negative consequences, but the reader should still know which side I consider more important overall.

    I do not currently have a personal Positive / Negative Development essay on this site. I therefore do not label any existing essay as my example for this type. The page links to a external model instead.

    This differs from a pure opinion essay, where I can take a strictly positive or strictly negative position and use both body paragraphs to support the same side.

    Structure & Task Response
    My pattern — both consequences, one net judgement
    ParaPurposeWhat I write
    IntroductionNet judgementParaphrase the development and state whether it is positive or negative overall.
    Body 1Positive sideDevelop the positive consequences with explanation and an example/mechanism.
    Body 2Negative sideDevelop the negative consequences, then make clear why the net judgement still holds.
    ConclusionFinal evaluationRestate the overall positive/negative judgement and the decisive reason.
    Language — Evaluation and Judgement
    Net judgement
    I consider this a largely positive development / a net negative / broadly beneficial overall
    Limiting side
    While X creates genuine coordination challenges, these are manageable through… / Although X poses a risk, it does not outweigh…
    Consequence evaluation
    The long-term effect is likely to outweigh the short-term cost because… / Its benefits in X, Y, and Z make the overall development positive
    Common Errors to Watch
    • TR
      Describing the change rather than evaluating its consequences. "More people now work from home" is description. "This reduces commuting time and broadens the talent pool employers can recruit from" is evaluation.
    • TR
      Net judgement absent or ambiguous. "Both sides have valid points" is not a judgement. State clearly: positive, negative, or mixed — and why.
